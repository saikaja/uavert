How scores work

Every place gets a score from 0 to 100. Higher means more reported risk. Scores are guidance based on public data, not a prediction or a guarantee; no place is ever labelled "safe".

Five parts, and the highest one counts

  • Crime: reported crimes from the Toronto Police Service, each weighted by how seriously Canadian courts treat it (Statistics Canada's Crime Severity Index), compared with a typical Toronto block or neighbourhood (see below).
  • Air quality: the current Air Quality Health Index from the nearest Environment Canada station.
  • Official alerts: Environment Canada's own colour sets the level: a yellow alert is moderate (40), orange elevated (65), red high (90). During a heat alert, the nearest City cool space open at that time is named.
  • News: recent news about a protest or violent incident nearby raises the score for 24 hours, at most to "elevated". News reports are unverified and labelled that way.
  • Crowds: on the dates of large City events (Nuit Blanche, Pride, the Caribbean Carnival, parades, New Year's Eve and others), places within about 1 km are at least moderate (35), with the event named. Big venues such as Rogers Centre are mentioned nearby but don't change the score, because their game and concert schedules aren't available to us.

The combined score is the highest of the five, never an average, so one serious risk is never hidden by several low ones. A walking route shows its worst block, and also what most of the walk is like.

What the numbers mean

Crime scores compare a place with a typical Toronto block (or, for neighbourhoods, a typical neighbourhood): the middle of the city, half above and half below. A typical place scores 25; every doubling adds 20 points.

Neighbourhood and street level

    Odds per resident

    Next to the score, each neighbourhood shows its odds: one reported incident a year for every N residents, for 2025, with Toronto's figure alongside for comparison. It is worked out as the neighbourhood's population divided by its reported incidents, at three levels of severity:

    LevelOffences
    High: serious violenceHomicides (averaged over 3 years), shootings, firearm offences, robberies, aggravated assaults and assaults with a weapon or causing bodily harm
    Medium: assaultCommon assault
    Low: property crimeBreak-ins, auto thefts, thefts over $5,000, thefts from vehicles and bicycle thefts

    Odds from 100 up are rounded to two figures (for example 1 in 450). They are not anyone's personal chance of being a victim: one incident can involve several people or none (a car theft), some crime isn't reported, and incidents involving visitors are counted against residents only, so busy areas with few residents show higher odds. Unlike street scores, the odds don't allow for foot traffic.

    Trends over time

    Under the odds, each neighbourhood shows how its reported crime per resident has changed over the last 10 years (2016–2025) or the last 5 (2021–2025), next to Toronto as a whole. The figures are Toronto Police's published yearly counts and rates for each neighbourhood, which allow for each year's population, so a neighbourhood that grew doesn't look worse just because more people live there.

    GroupOffences
    ViolentAssaults (all levels), robberies, homicides and shootings
    PropertyBreak-ins, auto thefts, thefts over $5,000, thefts from vehicles and bicycle thefts

    These groups differ from the odds levels because the yearly figures count all assaults together. The trendline is a straight line fitted through the yearly rates (least squares), and the percentage is how much that line changes from the first year to the last. A fitted line is less affected by one unusual year than comparing two single years. A change of 10% or more either way is called rising or falling, and anything smaller is roughly flat. If a neighbourhood averaged fewer than 10 incidents a year, it shows "too few to call a trend". The 5-year view starts in 2021, a pandemic year with less activity, so it can show a rise that partly reflects the return to normal.

    Allowing for crowds

    Busy streets have more reported incidents partly because far more people are there. Street scores therefore count incidents per person on foot, using City of Toronto pedestrian counts at intersections.

      Time of day

      Pick a time and street-level scores change with it. Two things change through the day: how often incidents happen near a block at that time, and how many people are out. Later at night, with fewer people around, the same incidents mean more risk for each person.

        Fairness check

        Crime scores can end up flagging poorer areas simply because they are poorer. Every time scores are rebuilt, Uavert checks how closely neighbourhood crime scores follow household income and the share of low-income households (2021 Census). If either relationship is strong (0.5 or more on a scale where 0 means none and 1 means scores rank exactly with income), the weights are flagged for review.

        Limitations

        • Crime data is what was reported to police. Toronto Police describes it as preliminary, and locations are moved to the nearest intersection for privacy.
        • Street scores allow for foot traffic, but the counts are daytime snapshots at intersections and can be several years old (the dates are shown with each score).
        • Neighbourhood rates use resident population, so areas with many visitors and few residents look higher.
        • Incidents recorded inside Toronto's two jails (Toronto South Detention Centre, 130 Horner Ave, and Toronto East Detention Centre, 55 Civic Rd) are left out of scores and odds. Toronto Police records them at the jail's address, and they aren't a risk to people in the neighbourhood. Incidents outside the jails still count.
        • A single serious incident (for example a homicide) can move a small neighbourhood a long way; it fades over the following months.
        • Toronto has only a few air quality stations, so air quality is similar across most of the city.
        • News is matched by keywords and is often missing a location; it is never treated as confirmed.
