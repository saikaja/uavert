# Phases 1–3 (addition): Odds alongside the risk score

**Task folder:** docs/work/2026-10-01-backend-foundation/  ·  **Date:** 2026-10-07  ·  **Status:** Approved

## What this step is
Define, design and plan one addition: next to the 0–100 risk score, show plain-language odds built from the real incident counts ("about 1 reported incident for every 84 residents a year"), split into low, medium and high severity. One approval covers all three phases.

## What was done
- Read the scoring code (`scoring/build.py`, `scoring/crime.py`, `scoring/bands.py`), the offence map (`data/offence_map.csv`), the CSI weights and the API that returns a place's score (`api/locate.py`).
- Queried the live database (Neon `main`) for 2025 incident counts by offence, neighbourhood populations (3,214,722 residents in total) and the street-cell foot-traffic estimates, to check that the odds can be calculated and what they look like.
- Found that `neighbourhood_scores.details` already stores, for each neighbourhood, the 2025 count of every offence group and its population. The odds can be calculated from data we already hold: no new sources, no database migration.

## 1. Definition

### The problem
A score of 53 "Elevated" tells people how a place compares, but not how likely anything is. Sai wants a figure people understand straight away, like "1 in 100", based on the actual data and split by how serious the incident is.

### What the user sees
For any searched address, a new **Odds** panel under the score:

> **Reported incidents in Yonge-Bay Corridor (2025), per resident**
> | Severity | This neighbourhood | Toronto |
> |---|---|---|
> | High: serious violence | 1 in 105 | 1 in 446 |
> | Medium: assault | … | 1 in 192 |
> | Low: property crime | … | 1 in 138 |
> | Any | 1 in 25 | 1 in 68 |
>
> *One reported incident a year for every N residents. Not a personal prediction: many incidents involve visitors, some aren't reported, and property crimes affect cars and homes rather than people. See how this is worked out.*

(Figures from the database on 2026-10-07; the "…" cells will be filled by the build. The Yonge-Bay "Any" figure above does not yet include thefts from vehicles and bicycle thefts, so the built figure will be a little lower.)

The neighbourhood panel (clicking a neighbourhood on the map) shows the same table. The rules page gets a short section explaining the calculation.

### Acceptance criteria
1. `/api/v1/risk-scores` and `/api/v1/neighbourhoods/{id}` return an `odds` object: for each of `high`, `medium`, `low` and `any`, the neighbourhood's "1 in N" and Toronto's "1 in N", the year, the population used and the counts behind them.
2. Each "1 in N" equals population ÷ reported incidents for 2025 (homicides as the 3-year yearly average, as in the score), rounded to 2 significant figures above 100 (for example "1 in 450", "1 in 1,200"). A severity with no incidents shows "None reported in 2025".
3. Toronto's figures for 2025 match the database counts: about 1 in 446 (high), 1 in 192 (medium), 1 in 138 (low), 1 in 68 (any), within rounding.
4. The panel is shown on the search result and on the neighbourhood panel, on laptop and phone widths, in light and dark themes.
5. Wording: it never says "safe", never says "chance of being a victim", and always names the year and "reported".
6. The risk score, bands and every existing reason are unchanged (existing tests pass; the acceptance check still passes).

### Out of scope (follow-ups)
- **Per-visit odds at street level** ("1 in 24,000 visits"). We have foot-traffic estimates, but the median block has only about 33 people an hour and most blocks have no count nearby, so per-visit odds would be mostly guesswork. Worth revisiting with better footfall data.
- Odds by time of day.
- Adjusting for unreported crime.

## 2. Design

### Severity levels
Grouped by harm to a person, using the offence groups already in the offence map:

| Level | Offences | Toronto 2025 count |
|---|---|---|
| **High: serious violence** | homicides (3-year average), shootings, firearm offences, robberies, assaults with a weapon or causing bodily harm, aggravated assaults | 7,211 |
| **Medium: assault** | common assault (assault level 1) | 16,708 |
| **Low: property crime** | break-ins, auto thefts, thefts over $5,000, thefts from vehicles, bicycle thefts | 23,282 |
| **Any** | all of the above | 47,201 |

The level for each offence becomes a new `severity` column in `data/offence_map.csv`, so it lives next to the rest of the offence rules and is easy to review.

### Calculation
- Per neighbourhood: `1 in N = population ÷ incidents` for 2025, for each level. This reuses the counts the neighbourhood score already computes, so the two can never disagree.
- Toronto: the same with city totals.
- Calculated in `uavert build-scores` and stored in `neighbourhood_scores.details.odds` (a JSON column that already exists). The API reads it; nothing is calculated per request.

### Why per resident, and the main weakness
"Per resident" is the only denominator that is real data for every neighbourhood (census population). Its weakness: places with many visitors and few residents look worse. Yonge-Bay Corridor has 16,661 residents but tens of thousands of daily visitors, so "1 in 25" overstates the odds for any one person there. The street score already corrects for this with foot traffic; the odds will not. The panel's note says so, and the Toronto column gives a fair comparison point.

### Assumptions (please correct any that are wrong)
1. Neighbourhood-level odds are acceptable even though the main score on a search is street-level. The panel is labelled with the neighbourhood's name so it's clear.
2. Common assault is "medium" and assault with a weapon or bodily harm is "high".
3. Robbery is "high" (it involves force or a threat against a person).
4. Break-ins count as "low" (property), even though some happen with people home.

### Alternatives considered
- **Grouping by CSI weight thresholds:** rejected. It puts assault causing bodily harm (weight 77) below break-ins (187), which reads wrongly for a personal-safety app.
- **"Your chance of being a victim":** rejected. Incidents aren't victims (one assault can have two victims; a car theft has none present), and it would claim more than the data shows.
